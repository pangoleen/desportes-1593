Doubts, by line:
r11, r14 M A G E B H C I "Mateouci" and M A G E H C I "Mateuci": the papal commissary Girolamo Matteucci.
r16 M A L H B I F I E "Malvoisie": Innocenzo Malvasia, his successor. Neither name was in the lexicon.
r12 "icy reste": S. Tomokiyo reads "en reste" (3 Oct 2026); the word has a descender, so "icy" is kept here.
r13, r17 clear words "de" and "de contredire a ung bruit" as read by S. Tomokiyo (3 Oct 2026), confirmed on the
    image; one short clear word after "bruit" is not read.
r24 D E F F E C F M: "deffecsz" for "d'effects" (opposed to "promesses").
r30 the lambda sign before "Gregoire": "pape" by context (Gregory XIV); "feu" is also possible.
r36 C A I E "clair" (an L short); the sign before "ne parlast" has the Pope's form; "elle" then = Sa Sainteté.
r41 A F F E H E E E: "asseuree" with one E too many, or "asseurer".
r51 between "que" and "aparances": G B + blot + ? E F / G E F. "toutes ces" by context.
v0-1 one glyph is lost in the gutter after "que"; A D H A at the start of l. 1 gives no clear word.
v2-3 B A + gutter / B H E before "de personnes": not read. "advertissemens" is what the glyphs give; the sense
     ("he neglects all their warnings") is not sure. The subject of "neglige ... prent coeur" is M. du Maine (r50).
v5  "pour faire le souverain et seul remede que sa presence": the glyphs are clear, the syntax is not.
v7-8 D E (struck glyphs) F A [gutter] / H C E G E F: "faucetés" = faussetés.
v9-10 G E C [gutter] / E: "gecter" (jeter).
v11-12 "de ce|ul" + blot + the *qui* sign: "de celuy qui"; context: the King of Spain.
v17 "ce qu'ilz ont font" as written (B A G, F B A G).
v24 F B A G + struck glyph + F E C B H E I E: "font [se] courir" or "sont secourir"; the first fits the context
    (the letters that the enemies offer to show, r27-28 and r43).
v27 "il me prendra pour luy" as written (M E); "ne" restored by sense.
v30 the sign after "aprehention de" has the Pope's form.

TRANSLATION (cipher passages)
[r11-13] ... at the cost of my head. From Monsieur Matteucci down to the one who remains here, they alone
have brought more ruin to our affairs than the enemy, so full have they been of I know not what that does not
suit us, or of small knowledge of our humours and passions. [r14-17] And if I put anything forward in the
discourse that I give to His Holiness about the conduct of the said Matteucci, the least letter that anyone
could write to me about it will always let me show it, wherever one wishes, to make it good. As for Monsieur
de Malvasia, I was not there when Monsieur du Maine and he fell out; yet the Legate's nephew complained to me
of it; and it is hard to contradict a rumour. [r17-20] And of herself Your Lordship can consider that the King of Spain's army did nothing
but stay on the frontier, a reproach to so great a monarch, and that it withdrew on a mere rumour that the
King of Navarre was coming to seek it. [r20-27] Moreover the Legate, with no consideration at all, wants us
to proceed to the election of a king at the moment when the enemy begins to win the voices of many who were
his enemies, by the show he makes of wishing to become Catholic; and he is to go to mass on Sunday. To have
nothing to resist him with but promises, by which we have been so often deceived! It would take many deeds
before we could be made to reject the appearance of a peace; and it will be no small thing if we temper it
by a truce, since everyone is tired of being the talk of all Christendom and of seeing ourselves abandoned,
only to be brought to bay in the end. [r27-32] For as to promises, our enemies do not lack them: they offer
to show letters of Your Most Illustrious Lordship [saying that], if he becomes Catholic, he will be received.
The Holy See is tired of continuing its expense, which may be said to have been something during the
pontificate of Pope Gregory; but since then it would have been better for it if we had been denied all hope
of help outright, so that we should take a firm resolution. [r32-39] Let Your Lordship remember how urgently
I asked for one of the house of Bourbon, and the refusal I was given; then, the marriage of the Infanta with
one of our princes: I was refused outright, and referred to the Legate and the Duke of Feria, to whom I
think I made it plain enough that we would never hazard our religion, honours and lives without other
assurance; and that unless His Holiness spoke more clearly (for we want no assurances but his, he being the
most concerned), no one would do better in future than in the past. I would have been most imprudent to act
otherwise, I who know the Spaniards. [r39-42] That is what made me reject the candidate that His Holiness
proposed to me, who in truth would have been the best; but one needed to see a sure light in it. And even
if my master had been willing to let himself go that way, no one would have resisted him more than I. And
where would he be now? [r42-48] The enemies offer, further, to show a letter of His Holiness addressed to
the Legate, by which he orders him to do nothing to the prejudice of the house of Bourbon against the
fundamental laws of the kingdom, nor against the Catholics who assist the King of Navarre: a thing quite
contrary to so many protestations made to me. I have said it and will say it until death: if harm comes of
it, it was willed so; for we did not fail to remonstrate all that could be said, with no passion but
religion. [r48-v6] But amid these uncertainties we remain; and if the others use the occasion, do they not
do well? And for reward of all our pains I expect a world of reproaches, all falling on Monsieur du Maine,
who does more than he should, without any fear; for honour is worth more than all these appearances that most
men seek; and the end will crown the work. For at the time when [...] is bent on tearing our state apart,
which would bring after it the ruin of Christendom, abandoned by everyone, he neglects all their warnings(?)
[...] of persons who, but for their quality of ministers of a great prince, would be neither out of rank nor
of too good a house to serve as servants; he takes heart against every kind of storm [...] the sovereign and
only remedy, his presence, to keep the state and religion from an imminent fall. [v6-15] There will surely
be some who, as their custom is, will cover their malice with a world of falsehoods; but if their design has
been made vain, there is no lack of persons able to write good apologies, and they will turn back the shame
that one would cast on the face of those on whom there is only the mark of honour and magnanimity. Would to
God His Holiness knew as I do how his ministers have behaved, their interest, and [that] of the one who makes
a show of being nothing but religious, whereas there is nothing but pure ambition, too plainly uncovered,
in spite of a number of protestations to the contrary; and to which there was no means of replying if the
Legate had not put himself in the matter, in which the honour of His Holiness has been greatly engaged.
[v15-23] I speak of his affairs to Your Lordship at great length; and if I did not fear to weary you, there
would be enough to fill a ream of paper. Not to be too tedious, I will say that if Your Lordship knew as I
do the artifice of our friends, what they do, their weakness, how the Legate has shown himself their partisan
to tear our poor state apart, to the prejudice of the honour of the Holy See, and a world of other things,
you would wonder a thousand times at the patience of Monsieur du Maine, who went so far as to be willing to
accept Monsieur de Guise as king, provided he was shown the means to defend him. But they want us to believe
in words, as if there were any reason to trust further people who never showed us that they had any, and whose
maxim is to deceive us. [v23-32] Perhaps you will find it strange that I have pointed out to you the two
points that the enemies put about, both from the letter of His Holiness and from yours in particular; but it
seemed good to me that you should be told everything, to provide for it. It is true, and I have protested it
often enough, that if it pleases His Holiness to let Monsieur du Maine know his intentions, without the
Legate between them, he will take only His commands for his rule. And things between those two are now such
that, but for respect for His Holiness, he would not stay so long at his own cost and at the ruin of all his
house. He [the Legate] has even been so disrespectful as to make him fear that he is in His Holiness's
disfavour; which everyone nonetheless thinks a device to reach his own ends, so as to oblige the Spaniard,
which has always been his main aim.
