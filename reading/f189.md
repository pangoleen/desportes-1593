## Working translation

> Our will was good, and the power great enough to make it succeed. But whether we could have held out in the long
> run, that is what made us stay [..]. And meanwhile our friends have shown themselves not merely neutral but
> outright enemies, and have done what they could to work the ruin of the [..] of the man of the College, and yours,
> sparing nothing [..], now on one pretext, now on another, seconded by our Doctor Gratian, who has played the part
> better than any of the others, to the point of staking the authority of his [..], compromising it and doing much
> harm. If our uncle had been willing to speak as he had promised the man who [went] to find him, and if the man of
> the chain had given other assurances than words, we would have gone in head down. But believe me, there is little
> to think of [..]. The best that could be done was protection and playing for time. It was not done. The truce was
> needed. The imprudence of the monarch and his people was such that he had made no preparation, or at least so
> little that the least gentleman in a quarrel would have made more. And for your [..], it was another fine act, the
> dismissal that your [..] made [..]. [I can hardly] believe what you write to me, that there is no change. Yet it is
> a strange thing that our [..] has brought us nothing back, and that we were told what they pleased, and it was only
> words, the strangest I would have thought [..]. To lose everything, even to be deprived of paradise, I would have
> borne much more [..]; to toil, to ruin oneself, to do what can be done, so that without our prudence things would
> not last an hour, and for reward to build a ruin. Now I would not be so charitable, I confess. If our friend of
> the College would speak of it to the man of the chain, he would infinitely oblige our friend, who is wholly his.
> And as for your pension, that cannot be, for nobody [supports] it but the prelate to whom the benefice is
> assigned, and it is not the time to [ask] him for anything. It has been your misfortune and mine. Courage: other
> occasions will come. I hold that we shall have the truce within a few days. The journey of [sign] has been the
> most harmful thing in the world. It has made [sign] change religion and offer [sign] thousand écus. Their party
> was ruined without this change. Our [..] legate has done what he could to help the Spaniard. He has embraced their
> cause with so much [..] that he has neglected the service of his [sign], to the point of not delivering a single
> one of our briefs, which would have helped infinitely. But that was not what he wanted; on the contrary, to
> hasten the dismemberment of our state. From the letter of [sign] you will know truly the general state of our
> affairs.

## Notes

- "Docteur Gratian" is the stock Doctor of the Italian comedy, so "nostre docteur Gratian" is probably a nickname
  for a person, as "l'homme de la chesne" and "l'homme du colege" are. These cover names are not yet identified.
- "Nostre [..] legat" is the papal legate in France, Filippo Sega, cardinal of Piacenza.
- The truce: the general truce of La Villette was agreed on 31 July 1593.
- Sign values taken from the deciphered sister letters (f. 177, f. 185) exist for the Pope, the King of Navarre,
  Mayenne, the King of Spain, the Legate, Guise and the Infanta. They are not yet applied to this page; see
  [../evidence/worklog.md](../evidence/worklog.md).
